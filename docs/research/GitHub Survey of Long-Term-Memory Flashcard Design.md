# GitHub Survey of Long-Term-Memory Flashcard Design

## Executive summary

The GitHub landscape does **not** reveal a single accepted formula for an ideal question-and-answer flashcard. It reveals a family of approaches that agree on several failure modes but disagree meaningfully about how aggressively to simplify knowledge, how much context to preserve, and how much reasoning should live on the graded answer side of a card. The most interesting repositories are therefore not the ones that merely call an LLM and export to Anki. They are the ones that treat card generation as a problem of **knowledge selection, decomposition, retrieval-task design, quality control, and later refinement**. citeturn5view0turn5view3turn18search2turn5view5

The strongest convergence is around **short, specific retrieval targets**. Skyfox's `lecture-to-anki`, maaarcooo's flashcard generator, LLMAnki, Recall, Anthropic's legal flashcard skill, and several other projects all discourage compound questions, ambiguous fronts, answer leakage, and unnecessarily verbose backs. They differ on terminology and strictness, but they are attacking essentially the same phenomenon: a card becomes difficult to interpret when success or failure could reflect several independent pieces of knowledge. citeturn6view0turn7view0turn5view4turn17search0turn2search20

That convergence should **not** be mistaken for evidence that every card must contain exactly one microscopic fact. Two of the more thoughtful GitHub treatments explicitly push back against rigid atomization. maaarcooo moved toward “flexible atomicity,” allowing naturally coupled information to stay together, while the `ankiweb-add-card` research report argues that atomicity is directional rather than absolute and warns that excessive decomposition can create context-free fragments. This tension is particularly relevant to programming, mathematics, and professional technical knowledge, where relationships, conditions, mechanisms, and tradeoffs are often the knowledge worth retaining. citeturn6view1turn7view0turn5view2

A second major finding is that **“what should become a card?” and “how should that card be worded?” are increasingly treated as separate problems**. Andy Matuschak makes this distinction explicitly in his writing on spaced-repetition prompts, and several newer GitHub projects operationalize it: AnkiThis first classifies material and extracts concepts and prerequisites before generating cards; `lecture-to-anki` uses learning objectives to determine coverage; `articles-to-anki` filters for core concepts before formulation; Recall separates fact extraction from card synthesis. This seems especially consequential for your use case because a knowledge professional's scarce resource is not the ability to manufacture questions. It is deciding which pieces of rarely used technical knowledge deserve years of future retrieval practice. citeturn4search13turn4search21turn18search2turn5view0turn12view4turn17search0

A third trend is that the better 2025-2026 projects increasingly distrust **one-shot generation**. They add reviewer agents, deterministic linters, semantic deduplication, source-provenance checks, or later reformulation based on failed reviews. `lecture-to-anki` has explicit lint rules; `anki-claude-cli` separates writing from reviewing; Recall performs iterative validation and semantic deduplication; Mneme includes heuristic rejection filters; AnkiAIUtils works in the opposite direction by improving cards that the learner actually fails. In other words, much of the interesting innovation on GitHub has moved from “write a clever prompt” to “construct a quality-control process around imperfect generation.” citeturn5view0turn5view5turn17search0turn18search1turn14search1

A fourth trend is **examples and personalization rather than ever-longer global instructions**. Voice2Anki retrieves examples from the learner's own previous corrections; AnkiAIUtils uses few-shot datasets; `lecture-to-anki` ships a complete worked run as an exemplar; maaarcooo explicitly reports that rigid stylistic rules proved brittle across model versions and therefore uses hard rules primarily to block defects while teaching softer style through examples. This is one of the clearest prompt-engineering lessons in the survey, but it remains a prompt-engineering lesson rather than a settled law of human memory. citeturn18search3turn13view1turn5view0turn6view1

Finally, the **learning-science foundation is unevenly distributed across these GitHub heuristics**. Retrieval practice itself and distributed practice have strong experimental and meta-analytic support. There is also evidence that retrieval can improve conceptual learning and transfer, although transfer is not automatic and can depend on the retrieval direction and task. By contrast, exact prescriptions such as “answers must contain at most five words,” “retrieval should take under ten seconds,” “one fact exactly,” or a particular Bloom's-taxonomy mapping are mostly practitioner conventions or project-specific heuristics, not conclusions directly established by the cited cognitive-science literature. citeturn16search0turn16search1turn16search2turn16search3turn16search7turn16search9

**For your stated use case, the survey's central unresolved question is not whether long cards are harder. GitHub overwhelmingly acts as though excessively broad cards are undesirable. The unresolved question is how much conceptual structure can be removed before a short card stops representing useful professional knowledge and becomes isolated trivia.** The most sophisticated projects recognize that tradeoff rather than pretending it disappears. citeturn6view0turn7view0turn5view2turn18search2

## Scope and shape of the GitHub landscape

I treated GitHub as the discovery layer and examined repositories, source files containing prompts/rubrics, Gists, an issue, a discussion-oriented proposal, curated “awesome” resources, and README-linked learning materials. I followed the projects' external references into SuperMemo, Anki documentation, Matuschak's notes, and primary or bibliographic records for retrieval-practice and spacing research. Activity dates below are GitHub commit dates where GitHub exposed them, checked against the public repository state on August 27, 2026. citeturn20view0turn20view1turn21view0turn21view1turn22view0turn19view0turn19view1

One useful distinction emerged quickly: **flashcard-generating software is much more common than flashcard-design methodology**. Many repositories solve document parsing, model invocation, Obsidian integration, or Anki export while saying relatively little about why the resulting questions should be memorable. For example, `obsidian-auto-anki` automates generation but its author explicitly describes being a beginner in spaced repetition and learning techniques, while DeckForge is candidly characterized by its author as primarily “vibe coding.” Those projects remain useful evidence about tooling, but they should not carry the same methodological weight as repositories that publish rubrics, prompts, examples, validation stages, or explicit theoretical rationales. citeturn12view1turn12view2

Likewise, I did not find one canonical GitHub repository functioning as *the* “awesome-flashcards” reference point. Instead there is an overlapping network: [`awesome-anki`](https://github.com/tianshanghong/awesome-anki) curates Anki resources and learning-method links; [`professional-programming`](https://github.com/charlax/professional-programming) contains a particularly relevant flashcard section for software professionals; technical-learning Gists collect SuperMemo, Janki, and programming-specific material; and repositories such as [`eth-anki`](https://github.com/XYQuadrat/eth-anki) expose actual CS-study practice influenced by the Twenty Rules. citeturn0search14turn1search0turn3search0turn0search24

For your purposes, that fragmented landscape is useful rather than unfortunate. It exposes at least four distinct traditions: the **SuperMemo/minimum-information tradition**, the **Matuschak prompt-as-retrieval-task tradition**, the **Anki practitioner tradition**, and the newer **LLM pipeline/quality-assurance tradition**. Current projects often mix all four, sometimes without cleanly separating empirical evidence from practitioner convention. citeturn4search0turn4search1turn4search2turn5view0turn6view1

## Common prompt patterns and card-design heuristics

**Atomicity or “one retrieval target.”** This is the most widespread pattern. Recall says one atomic fact; LLMAnki asks for one fact/concept; `anki-claude-cli` splits multi-fact responses; Anthropic's legal skill splits multi-element rules; Skyfox's rubric treats compound fronts as structural failures. The underlying operational concern is usually not merely that short cards “feel easier.” It is that a card containing independent subanswers makes the result difficult to score and, in an SRS, gives one scheduling decision to several pieces of knowledge that may have different strengths. citeturn17search0turn5view4turn5view5turn2search20turn6view0

A representative *paraphrased* transformation from this family is:

> **Broad:** “How does intervention X affect preload and afterload, and why?”  
> **Decomposed:** “How does X affect preload?” / “How does X affect afterload?”

Skyfox uses essentially this type of bad→good decomposition and explains that remembering one half but not the other should not generate a single undifferentiated review outcome. citeturn6view0

But GitHub also contains a useful corrective: **atomicity is a continuum, not necessarily a command to turn every relationship into disconnected atoms**. maaarcooo permits naturally coupled elements, while the `ankiweb-add-card` report warns that over-atomization can make a deck context-free. This distinction matters greatly in technical subjects. “TCP uses a three-way handshake” is a compact fact; “why exponential backoff reduces synchronized retry storms” is relational knowledge. Turning the latter into disconnected vocabulary fragments could preserve brevity while losing the thing a professional actually wants available years later. citeturn7view0turn5view2

**Generation rather than recognition.** Several prompts reject yes/no questions, obvious answer clues, or fronts that merely expose the target term. maaarcooo explicitly asks for production rather than recognition; LLMAnki checks whether the prompt allows guessing; Skyfox's rubric distinguishes a real recall question from recognition-style wording. This is consistent with the broader testing-effect literature in which active retrieval, rather than another exposure to the answer, is the learning event of interest. citeturn7view0turn5view4turn6view0turn16search0

For example, the GitHub pattern is closer to:

> “Which isolation level prevents dirty reads but may still permit non-repeatable reads?”

than:

> “Does Read Committed prevent dirty reads?”

The second cue gives away much more of the structure and collapses retrieval toward verification. That example is illustrative rather than copied from a repository, but it captures what multiple prompt sets mean by avoiding yes/no or recognition-heavy cards. citeturn7view0turn5view4

**A short answer with a precise grading boundary.** The projects differ on how short, but there is striking agreement that a flashcard answer should not routinely become an essay. Anthropic says a paragraph should generally be split; maaarcooo limits explanation-style responses to roughly a sentence or two; Recall adopts even more extreme word-count thresholds; Skyfox often moves rationale or caveats to non-graded extra material when they are not themselves the retrieval target. citeturn2search20turn7view0turn17search0turn6view0

This is directly relevant to the difficulty you described with long cards. GitHub's more methodology-conscious projects generally respond to a long answer by asking **whether several retrieval judgments have been bundled together**, not by searching for a magical phrasing that makes a paragraph easy to memorize. The important caveat is that the exact permissible answer length is not agreed upon. A formula and unit may belong together; a two-clause causal explanation may be one coherent conceptual unit; a five-item list may be too broad. citeturn7view0turn5view2

**Unambiguous, self-contained context without answer leakage.** Technical cards have an acute context problem. “What happens next?” is useless when separated from the source document; “In Raft leader election, what happens after…” is much more stable. Skyfox explicitly catches unanchored pronouns and missing qualifiers; LLMAnki requires enough connection to the original learning context; AnkiAIUtils reformulates cards toward self-contained phrasing. citeturn6view0turn5view4turn14search1

Yet more context is not automatically better. A cue can accidentally become a miniature explanation whose wording makes the answer obvious. This produces a recurring **context–cueing tension**: enough information to identify exactly which knowledge should be retrieved, but not so much that retrieval becomes pattern matching against the question itself. The better GitHub rubrics treat both ambiguous context and answer leakage as defects, which is more nuanced than simply commanding “make every card self-contained.” citeturn6view0turn5view4

**Separate explanation from the graded retrieval target.** This pattern is particularly visible in `lecture-to-anki`. A short answer can coexist with richer mechanism, mnemonic, provenance, or explanatory material placed outside the binary recall target. That is conceptually different from saying explanations do not matter. It says an explanation can be valuable during feedback without making the learner reproduce the entire explanation to earn a “correct” grade. citeturn5view0turn6view0

This is one point where GitHub projects diverge. AnkiThis deliberately generates higher-level analysis/evaluation questions for more advanced material, and `obsidian-flashcards-llm` explicitly accommodates long mathematical derivations. Those approaches put more complex cognition inside the retrieval task itself. Skyfox and maaarcooo more often try to preserve a crisp grading boundary. GitHub therefore contains at least two legitimate design philosophies: **flashcards as precise memory probes** versus **flashcards as repeated miniature reasoning exercises**. It does not provide decisive evidence that one should absorb the other. citeturn18search2turn14search2turn6view0turn7view0

**Selection before formulation.** This may be the most important pattern beyond atomicity. Matuschak argues that identifying what deserves reinforcement and writing a good prompt for that target are different problems; simply pointing a model at arbitrary prose and asking for prompts tends to produce weak results when the source does not itself make the important retrieval target obvious. citeturn4search13turn4search21

Several GitHub systems independently rediscover that separation. `lecture-to-anki` treats learning objectives as a specification; AnkiThis classifies the content and extracts concepts before planning cards; Recall has a fact-extraction stage; `articles-to-anki` explicitly filters trivia and over-specific examples. These architectures are quite different from “take every chunk and ask GPT for five cards.” citeturn5view0turn18search2turn17search0turn12view4

This becomes even more significant for professional retention. A medical board candidate has an externally defined syllabus. A software engineer trying to preserve knowledge of database internals, networking, compilers, security, mathematics, or obscure APIs does not. The problem is partly **future-value estimation**: which knowledge will be useful enough, yet infrequently exercised enough, to justify recurring reviews? GitHub projects can supply heuristics such as “core concept,” “learning objective,” “practical application,” or “prerequisite,” but none of the surveyed projects provides convincing evidence that an LLM can reliably predict the long-term future relevance of knowledge to an individual professional. citeturn12view4turn13view0turn18search2

**Prerequisites and hierarchy.** SuperMemo's older formulation advises understanding before memorizing and building from the whole toward parts; AnkiThis tracks prerequisite relationships explicitly, while the RemNote generator preserves hierarchical organization. This is one response to the over-atomization problem: small retrieval items can still exist inside a larger conceptual model rather than becoming a flat heap of disconnected facts. citeturn4search0turn18search2turn12view7

For mathematics and programming, this may be more consequential than it initially appears. A card about the contrapositive, amortized analysis, cache coherence, monadic composition, or database serializability can be perfectly concise and still be useless if the learner lacks the conceptual structure that gives the answer meaning. SuperMemo's “understand first” advice is old practitioner guidance, but newer content-adaptive GitHub projects are effectively reintroducing the same idea through prerequisite graphs and content classification. citeturn4search0turn18search2

**Multiple retrieval angles, but not mechanical reversal.** Matuschak repeatedly argues for accessing important ideas from multiple angles. There is also experimental reason to be cautious about assuming that success in one retrieval direction automatically transfers to another: Pan and Rickard found that retrieval benefits for one direction of paired information did not simply guarantee equivalent performance in the reversed direction. citeturn4search25turn16search7

GitHub implementations vary. AnkiThis automatically creates bidirectional vocabulary cards for factual terminology, whereas maaarcooo restricts reverse cards to cases such as key terms instead of mechanically reversing everything. For technical knowledge, the distinction is useful: recognizing that “CAP theorem” maps to a definition and being able to name the theorem from a described tradeoff may both matter; reversing a causal or explanatory question mechanically often produces nonsense. citeturn18search2turn7view0

**Deduplication and interference checks.** Semantic deduplication appears in Recall, Mneme, AnkiThis, `articles-to-anki`, and reviewer-oriented projects. maaarcooo also performs a second-pass check for interference and explicit contrasts. This reflects a newer LLM-era concern: automatic generation makes it easy to create enormous decks containing paraphrases of the same idea, near-duplicate cues, or cards whose answers compete because their fronts are insufficiently differentiated. citeturn17search0turn18search1turn18search2turn12view4turn6view1

There is an important evidential distinction here. It is obvious that deduplication can reduce **review workload**, because fewer cards must be reviewed. It is not established by these repositories that a particular embedding-similarity threshold maximizes long-term retention. Semantic deduplication is therefore a compelling knowledge-base-management idea whose exact memory consequences remain under-evaluated. citeturn17search0turn18search1

**Source fidelity and provenance.** This is particularly strong in professional domains. Skyfox checks whether claims are supported by the source; `anki-claude-cli` flags unverified claims; Anthropic's law skill keeps source references attached. This is not a mnemonic trick at all. It is an epistemic-safety property: a durable memory system is particularly dangerous if it efficiently consolidates a hallucinated simplification. citeturn5view0turn5view5turn2search20

For history, politics, medicine, standards, law, and rapidly changing technical systems, this distinction is critical. “Memorable” and “correct” are orthogonal properties. A system that makes an invented answer easy to retrieve has succeeded at memory engineering while failing at knowledge management. The GitHub projects that preserve provenance are therefore solving a problem that classic flashcard-design advice, written before LLM generation, had much less reason to emphasize. citeturn5view0turn2search20

**Examples over exhaustive stylistic law.** Voice2Anki retrieves a learner's corrected cards as demonstrations; AnkiAIUtils structures few-shot datasets; Skyfox includes a complete worked example; maaarcooo explicitly says style examples proved more robust than increasingly rigid global rules. citeturn18search3turn13view1turn5view0turn6view1

That is an especially interesting result for a private system because it suggests that “good flashcard style” may partly be **learner-specific and corpus-specific**. A concise card written for someone who already understands operating-system scheduling should look different from one written for someone first encountering threads. GitHub's personalization-oriented projects treat previous accepted/corrected cards as evidence about that difference rather than assuming the prompt author can enumerate every stylistic exception in advance. citeturn18search3turn13view1

**Failure-driven reformulation.** The Anki manual's treatment of leeches says persistent failure can indicate excessive information or insufficient understanding and recommends changing the item's presentation or addressing related concepts. AnkiAIUtils turns that traditional manual practice into an LLM workflow by revisiting cards the learner actually struggles with. citeturn4search2turn14search1

This creates an important contrast with pre-generation quality scores. A reviewer agent can predict that a question is ambiguous; only actual review history can reveal that a seemingly excellent cue repeatedly fails for one particular learner. GitHub therefore contains both **ex ante quality control** and **ex post adaptation**, and there is no reason to assume they identify exactly the same problems. citeturn5view5turn14search1

## What learning science supports, and what remains practitioner lore

The strongest empirical foundation beneath this ecosystem is **retrieval practice itself**. Roediger and Karpicke's work found that testing material after study substantially improves later retention relative to additional study under delayed testing conditions. Dunlosky and colleagues' broad review rated practice testing and distributed practice among the highest-utility learning techniques they considered. citeturn16search0turn16search9

There is also substantial evidence for **spacing**. Cepeda and colleagues' quantitative synthesis covered hundreds of experiments and found a robust distributed-practice advantage, while subsequent work showed that the spacing interval interacts with how long information must ultimately be retained. This supports the general premise of combining retrieval cards with spaced scheduling, but it does not tell us exactly how verbose a card's answer should be. citeturn16search1turn16search5

The evidence is not restricted to bare vocabulary recall. Karpicke and Blunt reported that retrieval practice enhanced meaningful learning from science texts and performance on inference questions, which is relevant to the idea that repeated retrieval can support conceptual learning rather than only rote fact retention. A published comment challenged aspects of that study's interpretation and comparison with concept mapping, so it should not be treated as a solitary definitive demonstration. Broader work nevertheless supports retrieval practice as a powerful learning intervention. citeturn16search2turn16search6turn16search9

Transfer is **real but not unlimited**. Pan and Rickard's meta-analytic work found retrieval-practice transfer across unpracticed contexts, but transfer magnitude varies substantially. Their directional work is particularly relevant to flashcards because retrieving A→B need not make B→A equally retrievable. That gives some scientific support to Matuschak's intuition that important ideas may deserve more than one retrieval route, while also arguing against the simplistic notion that every card should merely be auto-reversed. citeturn16search3turn16search7

The science becomes less direct when GitHub reaches **minimum information and atomicity**. Wozniak's Twenty Rules are enormously influential and grounded in long experience with SuperMemo. They advise understanding material before memorizing it, building from fundamentals, applying the minimum-information principle, and making retrieval items simple. However, “one fact per card” in the extremely strict sense used by some newer repositories should be understood as a practitioner design principle extrapolated into software architecture, not as a universally quantified result from a controlled experiment demonstrating one optimal information count for every domain. citeturn4search0turn17search0

This distinction matters because the word **“atomic” hides several different ideas**:

| Meaning of “atomic” | How GitHub uses it | Evidential status |
|---|---|---|
| Only one independently gradable retrieval target | Skyfox, `anki-claude-cli`, LLMAnki, Anthropic legal skill. citeturn6view0turn5view5turn5view4turn2search20 | Strong practical rationale for SRS grading; consistent with precise retrieval, but no universal laboratory threshold for “one target.” |
| Extremely short cue/response | Recall's ≤10-word cue and ≤5-word response are an extreme example. citeturn17search0 | Project-specific operational heuristic. |
| No unnecessary information | SuperMemo's minimum-information tradition. citeturn4search0 | Longstanding practitioner principle, broadly compatible with reducing retrieval ambiguity. |
| Preserve natural conceptual coupling | maaarcooo's flexible atomicity. citeturn7view0 | Engineering judgment intended to avoid pathological splitting. |
| Avoid context-free fragments | `ankiweb-add-card` report. citeturn5view2 | Important practitioner critique; direct comparative retention evidence remains limited. |

There is similarly little basis for treating **specific numeric card limits** as cognitive constants. Recall's word-count and predicted-retrieval-time limits are useful as an aggressively minimalist design stance, but its README's precision should not be confused with precision in the underlying science. The external research it cites establishes retrieval and spacing effects much more convincingly than it establishes those exact numerical cutoffs. citeturn17search0turn16search0turn16search1

**Bloom's taxonomy-driven generation** is another area where GitHub implementation gets ahead of direct evidence. AnkiThis's adaptation is intellectually interesting: simple factual content gets recall-oriented cards while advanced material can receive analysis or evaluation prompts. But the fact that a question can be classified as “higher-order” does not by itself establish that putting that task on a recurring flashcard yields better multi-year retention of technical knowledge. It may instead change what cognitive skill the card practices. citeturn18search2

Likewise, **semantic deduplication, LLM reviewer agents, vector-based interference detection, and few-shot personalization** are largely engineering innovations rather than interventions with a mature learning-science literature specific to flashcard generation. They can be compelling for obvious operational reasons, such as reducing redundant reviews, catching unsupported claims, and matching an individual's preferred wording, without having been independently shown to increase a particular retention metric. citeturn17search0turn5view5turn18search3

One especially useful point of agreement between modern GitHub projects and older learning theory is that **difficulty is not necessarily evidence of desirable learning**. The Anki leech documentation explicitly warns that repeated failure may mean a card contains too much information or rests on insufficient understanding. SuperMemo similarly starts with understanding before memorization. That is different from the broader concept of desirable difficulties: a useful retrieval effort is not the same thing as struggling to decode what the card is asking. citeturn4search2turn4search0

The science also argues against one simplistic reading of the testing effect. Soderstrom and colleagues found that when spacing was carefully controlled, both repeated testing and repeated study could produce benefits, emphasizing interactions between retrieval and temporal distribution. The broad case for retrieval remains strong, but claims such as “every additional explanatory exposure is useless once retrieval exists” go beyond the evidence. citeturn16search11

Taken together, the evidence hierarchy looks roughly like this: **retrieval practice and spacing are robust; matching retrieval practice to the desired future retrieval route has meaningful support; precise, unambiguous cues are strongly motivated by both theory and practice; minimum-information/atomic cards are influential and plausible but less directly parameterized by experimental evidence; exact word limits, Bloom mappings, embedding thresholds, and particular LLM review architectures are current engineering hypotheses.** citeturn16search0turn16search1turn16search3turn16search7turn4search0turn17search0

## Synthesis for technical knowledge professionals

The most useful way to synthesize the GitHub landscape is not as a list of commandments, but as a set of **design tensions on which projects make different choices**.

| Design axis | One end of the GitHub spectrum | Other end | Why it matters for technical/professional memory |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Granularity** | Strict one-fact minimalism: Recall, many MIP-inspired generators. citeturn17search0 | Flexible conceptual units: maaarcooo and the `ankiweb-add-card` report. citeturn7view0turn5view2 | Programming/math knowledge often lives in relationships, conditions, tradeoffs, and mechanisms. Excessive bundling makes grading noisy; excessive splitting can destroy meaning. |
| **Retrieval complexity** | Crisp factual/conceptual answer: Skyfox, Anthropic legal skill. citeturn6view0turn2search20 | Repeated analysis/derivation: AnkiThis and `obsidian-flashcards-llm`. citeturn18search2turn14search2 | A professional may want either durable declarative access or recurring problem-solving practice. They are related but not identical objectives. |
| **Context** | Minimal cue. | Strongly self-contained cue with source framing. LLMAnki and AnkiAIUtils explicitly emphasize contextual recognizability/self-containment. citeturn5view4turn14search1 | Old cards must still make sense years after the original textbook or codebase has disappeared from working memory, but too much context can leak the answer. |
| **Card selection** | Generate uniformly from chunks. | Explicit importance/concept/prerequisite selection: AnkiThis, `articles-to-anki`, Skyfox. citeturn18search2turn12view4turn5view0 | Review time compounds over years. A technically flawless card about an irrelevant detail may still be a bad long-term investment. |
| **Quality control** | One-shot prompt. | Linter/reviewer/refinement/dedup pipeline: Skyfox, Recall, `anki-claude-cli`, Mneme. citeturn5view0turn17search0turn5view5turn18search1 | Automatic generation makes low-quality cards cheap to create, so generation scale increases the importance of rejecting them. |
| **Personalization** | Universal rules. | Learner-specific examples/corrections: Voice2Anki and AnkiAIUtils. citeturn18search3turn13view1 | Prior knowledge changes which cue is sufficient and which vocabulary is natural. |
| **When quality is judged** | Before first review through an LLM rubric. | After observed failures through reformulation: AnkiAIUtils/Anki leeches. citeturn14search1turn4search2 | Some card defects are predictable from text; others become visible only through an individual's retrieval history. |
| **Epistemic grounding** | Trust model-generated summary. | Preserve source provenance and reject unsupported claims: Skyfox, `anki-claude-cli`, Anthropic legal skill. citeturn5view0turn5view5turn2search20 | Long-term memory magnifies the cost of hallucination because incorrect generated material can become fluently retrievable. |

For **programming and computer science**, the repositories suggest a distinction between knowledge that is naturally answerable in a small unit and knowledge that is inherently relational. API invariants, command semantics, protocol constants, terminology, algorithmic complexity, and crisp conditions often fit the minimalist tradition. Architecture tradeoffs, causal mechanisms, correctness arguments, debugging models, and “why” questions push toward the flexible-atomicity or explanation-oriented end. AnkiThis's explicit treatment of technical documentation as a distinct source type is unusual and useful evidence that generic textbook prompting is not the only model available. citeturn18search2turn7view0turn5view2

For **mathematics**, GitHub exposes a particularly important fault line. A tool can technically generate a card whose answer is a long derivation, as `obsidian-flashcards-llm` advertises, but the minimum-information projects would generally decompose or relocate much of that reasoning. Neither approach alone answers whether the desired durable capability is remembering a theorem's conditions, recognizing when it applies, recalling a proof idea, or reproducing a derivation. Those are different retrieval tasks, and Matuschak's broader prompt philosophy is useful here because it frames a prompt as a deliberate task for the future self rather than merely a compressed passage from the source. citeturn14search2turn4search1turn4search32

For **medicine**, the GitHub material is richer because several authors have used these tools in medical-study contexts. Skyfox's rubric includes medicine-like domain patterns; Voice2Anki and AnkiAIUtils have direct medical-school provenance; the atomicity tradition maps naturally onto separate attributes such as mechanism, indication, contraindication, adverse effect, and diagnostic implication. But the same corpus also illustrates why rigid atomization is incomplete: mechanism and clinical interpretation often require causal or conditional context. citeturn6view0turn13view2turn12view6

For **history and politics**, provenance arguably rises in importance. A card can be grammatically perfect and memorably concise while flattening a contested interpretation or silently converting an uncertain claim into a fact. The most relevant GitHub pattern is therefore not merely atomicity but the source-fidelity machinery in Skyfox, `anki-claude-cli`, and Anthropic's professional-domain skill. Those systems provide a useful contrast with generic “summarize this paragraph into cards” prompts. citeturn5view0turn5view5turn2search20

For your broader target population of **knowledge professionals retaining information they do not frequently use at work**, GitHub's most interesting contribution may be the recognition that rarity of natural use is exactly what makes deliberate retrieval valuable, while also making card selection expensive. Spacing research supports long-term distributed retrieval; professional-programming communities have explicitly adopted SRS for technical knowledge; but the ecosystem has much less mature thinking about deciding which information deserves to consume recurring attention for years. citeturn16search1turn16search5turn1search0turn3search0

That leaves a notable research gap. Modern repositories are becoming sophisticated about **how to transform selected information into cards**, but relatively few are sophisticated about **whether the information should enter long-term scheduled memory at all**. Learning objectives, concept importance, prerequisites, practical relevance, and content density are proxies. None is equivalent to “this piece of knowledge is sufficiently valuable, sufficiently durable, insufficiently reinforced by normal work, and sufficiently retrievable to justify ongoing maintenance.” citeturn5view0turn18search2turn12view4turn13view0

There is a second gap around **longitudinal validation**. Repositories frequently describe outputs as “optimized,” “scientifically optimized,” “high quality,” or “research-based,” but very few publish controlled comparisons connecting their specific card-generation choices to months- or years-later retention. Mneme deserves credit for explicitly marking its hand-graded benchmark as not yet run. Most other projects provide prompts, examples, author experience, lint criteria, or theoretical citations rather than longitudinal outcome data. citeturn18search1turn17search0turn18search2

A third gap is **professional transfer**. Retrieval practice can transfer beyond identical test items, but transfer is variable, and directional retrieval research shows that recalling one mapping does not guarantee access through every future cue. For a professional system, remembering “the definition” may matter less than being able to retrieve the concept when a production incident, design review, medical presentation, mathematical problem, or historical argument presents an unfamiliar cue. GitHub projects such as Matuschak-influenced generators and AnkiThis are beginning to address this through multiple angles, comparisons, mechanisms, and content-aware questions, but the field has not converged on how much such transfer-oriented variety is worth relative to the additional review load. citeturn16search3turn16search7turn4search25turn18search2

And there is a fourth gap around **the boundary between memory and understanding**. SuperMemo says to understand before memorizing; minimum-information systems then attempt to make the retrievable units small. More recent GitHub projects sometimes try to put understanding back inside the card through “why,” mechanism, comparison, Bloom-level analysis, or practical-application questions. The unresolved issue is whether such understanding should itself be repeatedly produced as the graded answer, or whether short retrieval cues should instead activate a conceptual structure built elsewhere. Both philosophies are visible in the surveyed repositories. citeturn4search0turn6view0turn18search2turn13view0

So the GitHub survey does **not** support a single ideal flashcard format. It supports a more interesting picture: good Q&A flashcards are being treated as deliberately designed retrieval tasks whose **scope, cue, grading boundary, context, provenance, relationship to prerequisite knowledge, redundancy with other cards, and behavior under actual review** can all be independently examined. The projects differ substantially in where they draw those boundaries. citeturn4search1turn5view0turn6view1turn5view4turn14search1

The closest thing to consensus is negative rather than prescriptive: **avoid cards where failure is hard to interpret**. A long answer containing six independent claims, an ambiguous front, a yes/no cue, an answer leaked by wording, an unsupported generated claim, a context-free fragment, and three semantic duplicates are different pathologies, but they all weaken the relationship between “I passed/failed this review” and “I can/cannot retrieve this particular knowledge when I need it.” The GitHub projects disagree productively about how small and sophisticated the resulting card should be once those pathologies are removed. citeturn6view0turn7view0turn5view4turn5view5turn17search0

That disagreement is probably the most useful result of the survey for a private learning system. **Long-term-memory flashcard design on GitHub is not converging toward one magic prompt. It is converging toward explicit tradeoffs, quality checks, and a separation between content selection and retrieval-task formulation, while leaving granularity and conceptual depth genuinely open.** citeturn4search13turn5view0turn6view1turn18search2turn5view2
